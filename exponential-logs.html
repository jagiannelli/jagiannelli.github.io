    Section 5.2 Log Graphs and Fitting Data

    A curve is hard to read a formula from, but a straight line is easy. Taking logarithms turns two common kinds of relationship into straight lines. A power law \(y = ax^n\) becomes
    \begin{equation*} \log y = n\log x + \log a, \end{equation*}
    which is \(Y = mX + c\) with \(Y = \log y\) and \(X = \log x\text{:}\) a straight line of gradient \(n\) and intercept \(\log a\text{.}\) An exponential \(y = kb^x\) becomes
    \begin{equation*} \log y = x\log b + \log k, \end{equation*}
    a straight line when \(\log y\) is plotted against \(x\) itself, with gradient \(\log b\) and intercept \(\log k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Any base of logarithm works, as long as the same one is used all the way through. Sage’s log is the natural logarithm, and log(v, 10) gives base 10, which this page uses for the numbers. The cells share their variables, so run them in order from the top.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.2 The curve and its log graph: two sliders

    In each picture the blue curve is on the left and its log graph is on the right in red. Click on a slider handle, then use the arrow keys to move one step at a time. First the power law \(y = ax^n\text{,}\) plotted as \(\log y\) against \(\log x\text{.}\) It starts at \(y = 3x^{1.5}\text{.}\) The line always has gradient \(n\text{,}\) and \(a\) only moves it up or down. Try \(n = 1\text{:}\) the curve is already a straight line, and its log graph has gradient 1.
    🔗
    Now the exponential \(y = kb^x\text{,}\) plotted as \(\log y\) against \(x\text{.}\) It starts at \(y = 2 \times 1.5^x\text{.}\) Setting \(b = 1\) gives a flat line, since \(\log 1 = 0\text{,}\) and \(b\) below 1 is decay, which gives a negative gradient.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.3 Estimating a and n from data

    Real measurements never lie exactly on a line, so the work goes the other way: plot the logs of the data, fit a line, and read the constants off it. The numbers below are invented, loosely imitating the pattern biologists find between an animal’s body mass (kg) and the power it uses at rest (watts). They suggest \(\text{rate} = a\,\text{mass}^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    It is a curve. Plot \(\log(\text{rate})\) against \(\log(\text{mass})\text{.}\) If the points lie close to a line, the power law fits, and the line gives \(n\) (its gradient) and \(\log a\) (its intercept). By hand you would draw the best line by eye and read two points off it. find_fit finds the best line for us:

    The gradient is \(n\text{,}\) and \(a\) is ten to the power of the intercept, since the intercept is \(\log a\text{.}\) The fitted law can then predict a mass that was not measured:
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.4 Which graph is straight?

    With data in hand you may not know whether to plot against \(\log x\) or against \(x\text{.}\) Try both and see which is straighter. The correlation coefficient \(r\) measures it: it is \(1\) or \(-1\) for a perfect line and nearer \(0\) for a scatter. (The yeast count at hour 0 has no \(\log x\text{,}\) so that point is left out of the second graph.)
    🔗
    The masses and rates are much straighter against \(\log x\text{,}\) and the yeast counts against \(x\text{,}\) which agrees with the models we chose. A high \(r\) alone proves nothing, though. The yeast counts also score highly against \(\log x\text{,}\) so look at the picture as well as the number.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.5 Where an exponential model stops working

    A model describes the range of the data it was fitted to, and no further. Growth cannot go on for ever. Run the fitted yeast model to 48 hours:
    🔗
    That is over a billion cells in one cubic millimetre, more than could physically fit. Food and space run out first, and a refinement would level the growth off.
    🔗
    Decay has a floor in real life. A cup of tea at \(80\) degrees Celsius in a room at \(20\) degrees does not cool towards \(0\text{.}\) Newton’s law of cooling says the rate of cooling is proportional to the difference from the room, so it is that difference which decays exponentially. Sage solves it, and the \(20\) comes out as a floor:
    🔗
    Compare it with a plain exponential from \(80\text{,}\) using \(k = 0.08\) per minute. The dashed curve heads for \(0\text{,}\) which the tea cannot reach. The red curve levels off at \(20\text{:}\)
    🔗
