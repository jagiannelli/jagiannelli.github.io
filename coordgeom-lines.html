    Section 4.1 Straight Lines

    A straight line is fixed by one point and a gradient. The gradient of the line through \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) is the increase in \(y\) over the increase in \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}. \end{equation*}
    Nearly everything else on this page (equations of lines, where lines meet, lengths, areas and angles) is built from that one number and some simultaneous equations. Sage keeps every answer exact and draws the picture.
    🔗
    The cells on this page share their variables, so run them in order from the top. The helper functions in the first subsection are used all the way down.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.1 Some helper functions

    We work with \(x\) and \(y\) throughout, so declare them first. The %display latex line makes Sage typeset every answer from here on.
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.1 Utility methods for points and lines.

    The gradient, length and midpoint of \(PQ\text{;}\) the line through \(P\) with gradient \(m\text{,}\) written as \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\text{;}\) the same line rearranged as \(y = mx + c\text{;}\) and the point where two lines meet, found by solving them as simultaneous equations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.2 Utility methods general_form() and general_equation().

    The form \(ax + by + c = 0\) with whole numbers, no common factor, and \(a\) positive (or \(b\) positive when there is no \(x\) term). general_form() returns the three numbers and general_equation() the equation itself.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.3 Utility methods signed_term(), line_text() and surd_text().

    Text versions for printing: one term of an equation with its sign, a line as x + 2y - 7 = 0, and the square root of a whole number in its simplest surd form, so that surd_text(45) gives 3√5.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.4 Utility methods diagram() and area_check().

    A picture drawn to scale: lines given as equations (a vertical line is drawn on its own, since it has no \(y = \dots\) form), dashed segments between points, and named points labelled with their coordinates. Then the area of any triangle from its three corners, which is for checking answers rather than for working them out.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.2 Perpendicular gradients multiply to \(-1\)

    Turn a line through a right angle and the step "across \(p\text{,}\) up \(q\)" becomes "across \(-q\text{,}\) up \(p\)". The gradient goes from \(q/p\) to \(-p/q\text{,}\) and the product of the two is \(-1\text{.}\) That is the test for perpendicular lines, and it is how to find the gradient of a perpendicular: take \(-1\) over the gradient you have.
    🔗
    Move \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\) The blue line is \(AB\text{,}\) the red line is perpendicular to it through \(B\text{,}\) and \(M\) is the midpoint of \(AB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Things to try. Make \(AB\) steeper and watch the red line get flatter: the gradients multiply to \(-1\) every time. Put \(A\) and \(B\) at the same height and the red line turns vertical. That is the one case where the product test can’t be used, because a vertical line has no gradient. Swap \(A\) and \(B\text{:}\) the gradient of \(AB\) stays the same.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.6 The angle between two lines

    A line with gradient \(m\) makes the angle \(\tan^{-1} m\) with the \(x\)-axis. The angle between two lines is the difference between their two angles. Here are \(PQ\) (gradient \(-\frac12\)) and the line \(y = \frac13 x + 1\text{,}\) in degrees:
    🔗
    The difference is exactly \(45^\circ\text{.}\) Two lines that cross make two angles, which add up to \(180^\circ\text{,}\) so the other one is \(135^\circ\text{.}\) The difference of the \(\tan^{-1}\) values gives one of them, and the diagram shows which one you want. The exact answer comes from the tangent of a difference:
    \begin{equation*} \tan(\alpha - \beta) = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{1 + m_1 m_2}. \end{equation*}
    This formula also shows why perpendicular lines have \(m_1 m_2 = -1\text{:}\) the bottom is zero there, and \(\tan 90^\circ\) is undefined.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.7 A straight line as a model

    A candle is 21 cm tall after burning for 1 hour, and 12 cm tall after 4 hours. If it burns at a steady rate, its height \(h\) after \(t\) hours lies on the line through \((1, 21)\) and \((4, 12)\text{.}\) The cell shows the rate, the model, and when the candle burns down to nothing:
    🔗
    The gradient \(-3\) is the rate of burning, in cm per hour. The intercept \(24\) is the height before it was lit, which neither reading gave directly. The model says the candle is gone after 8 hours. A model like this is only good over a range. In practice the wick and the stub at the bottom stop it before zero, and the line would carry on to negative heights if we let it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.8 The ratio in which a line divides a segment

    The line \(L: 3x + y - 1 = 0\) crosses the segment from \(A(-4, -1)\) to \(B(8, 5)\) at a point \(C\text{.}\) Moving along a straight line, the \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates change in proportion. So the ratio \(AC : CB\) can be read from the \(x\)-steps, or from the \(y\)-steps, without working out any lengths:
    🔗
    The \(x\)-steps are \(4\) and \(8\text{,}\) the \(y\)-steps are \(2\) and \(4\text{,}\) and the lengths are in the same ratio: \(AC : CB = 1 : 2\text{.}\)
