    Section 4.2 Rational Powers and Series

    When \(n\) is a positive integer, the product in the combination formula
    \begin{equation*} \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n(n - 1)\cdots(n - r + 1)}{r!} \end{equation*}
    contains the factor \(n - n = 0\) whenever \(r > n\text{,}\) terminating the expansion at \(n + 1\) terms. But when \(n\) is negative or fractional (\(n \in \mathbb{Q}\)), the numerator never reaches zero. The expansion continues indefinitely as an infinite series:
    \begin{equation*} (1 + x)^n = 1 + nx + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2!}x^2 + \frac{n(n - 1)(n - 2)}{3!}x^3 + \dots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    An infinite series does not always sum to a finite value. The binomial series converges if and only if \(|x| < 1\text{.}\) For an expression \((1 + kx)^n\text{,}\) replacing \(x\) with \(kx\) requires \(|kx| < 1\text{,}\) giving the range of validity \(|x| < \dfrac{1}{|k|}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This section investigates how term magnitudes grow and shrink in a finite expansion to find the greatest coefficient, develops infinite binomial series for fractional powers in Sage, applies series to find exact rational approximations for surds, and provides diagnostic practice questions.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.1 The Greatest Coefficient in an Expansion

    In the expansion of \((a + bx)^n\text{,}\) the terms do not grow or shrink indefinitely: they rise to a peak and then fall. To locate this peak without evaluating every term, examine the ratio of consecutive term magnitudes:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{|T_{r+1}|}{|T_r|} = \frac{\binom{n}{r+1} a^{n-r-1} |b|^{r+1}}{\binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} |b|^r} = \frac{n - r}{r + 1} \frac{|b|}{a}. \end{equation*}
    The terms grow in magnitude as long as this ratio exceeds 1:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{n - r}{r + 1} \frac{|b|}{a} > 1 \iff (n - r)|b| > (r + 1)a. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Consider \((2 - 5x)^8\text{.}\) Here \(n = 8, a = 2, b = -5\text{.}\) The condition for growth is:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{8 - r}{r + 1} \times \frac{5}{2} > 1 \iff 5(8 - r) > 2(r + 1) \iff 40 - 5r > 2r + 2 \iff 7r < 38 \iff r < \frac{38}{7} \approx 5.43. \end{equation*}
    Thus the magnitudes grow for \(r = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\text{,}\) peaking at \(r = 6\text{,}\) and shrink thereafter. Because \(b = -5\) is negative, terms with even \(r\) are positive and terms with odd \(r\) are negative. Since \(r = 6\) is even, it is positive and yields the greatest coefficient in the expansion:
    \begin{equation*} T_6 = \binom{8}{6} 2^2 (-5)^6 = 28 \times 4 \times 15625 = 1{,}750{,}000. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.3 Surd Approximations via Binomial Series

    Binomial series provide a systematic method for approximating irrational roots with fractions. Consider expanding
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \frac{2 - x}{\sqrt{1 + 3x}} = (2 - x)(1 + 3x)^{-1/2} \end{equation*}
    up to the term in \(x^2\text{.}\) First expand \((1 + 3x)^{-1/2}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} (1 + 3x)^{-1/2} = 1 + \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)(3x) + \frac{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)}{2!} (3x)^2 + \dots = 1 - \frac{3}{2}x + \frac{27}{8}x^2 + \dots \end{equation*}
    Multiplying by \((2 - x)\) and discarding terms of degree higher than 2:
    \begin{equation*} (2 - x)\left(1 - \frac{3}{2}x + \frac{27}{8}x^2\right) = 2 - 3x + \frac{27}{4}x^2 - x + \frac{3}{2}x^2 = 2 - 4x + \frac{33}{4}x^2. \end{equation*}
    The expansion is valid for \(|3x| < 1 \iff |x| < \frac{1}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now evaluate both sides at \(x = \frac{1}{22}\text{,}\) which lies comfortably within the validity interval \(|x| < \frac{1}{3}\text{:}\)
    • The left-hand side is:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{2 - 1/22}{\sqrt{1 + 3/22}} = \frac{43/22}{\sqrt{25/22}} = \frac{43/22}{5/\sqrt{22}} = \frac{43\sqrt{22}}{110}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • The right-hand side is:
      \begin{equation*} 2 - 4\left(\frac{1}{22}\right) + \frac{33}{4}\left(\frac{1}{22}\right)^2 = 2 - \frac{2}{11} + \frac{33}{1936} = \frac{323}{176}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    Equating the two yields a rational approximation for \(\sqrt{22}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{43\sqrt{22}}{110} \approx \frac{323}{176} \implies \sqrt{22} \approx \frac{323 \times 110}{176 \times 43} = \frac{1615}{344} \approx 4.69477. \end{equation*}
    The true value is \(\sqrt{22} \approx 4.69042\text{,}\) so just two terms of the series achieve an error of under \(0.0044\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
