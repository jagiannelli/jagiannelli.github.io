    Section 5.1 Growth, Decay and Doubling

    Some quantities change at a rate proportional to their own size. A radioactive sample decays faster when there is more of it, and a savings account earns interest on its own interest. If \(y\) is such a quantity at time \(t\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = ky \end{equation*}
    for a constant \(k\text{,}\) and the solution is \(y = Ae^{kt}\text{.}\) At \(t = 0\) this is \(Ae^0 = A\text{,}\) so \(A\) is the starting value. If \(k > 0\) the quantity grows, and if \(k < 0\) it decays.
    🔗
    Every such curve has a fixed doubling time (growth) or half-life (decay), however large or small the quantity has become: \(e^{kT} = 2\) gives \(T = \dfrac{\ln 2}{k}\text{.}\) This page shows where the model comes from, how to find \(A\) and \(k\) from two readings, how a percentage rate turns into a power, and how to solve for a time. The cells share their variables, so run them in order from the top.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.1 Why the model is exponential

    Declare the letters first. The %display latex line makes Sage typeset every answer from here on. Cells that mix words with formulas typeset them too, with show(LatexExpr(...)). Sage writes the natural logarithm \(\ln\) as \(\log\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The equation \(dy/dt = ky\) is a first-order differential equation, and the First Order Differential Equations section shows how desolve() works. Here it only needs to confirm that the answer is exponential. The arbitrary constant _C in the general solution is the starting value, which the second call fixes by saying that \(y = A\) when \(t = 0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Now check the claim about doubling. Start the clock at any moment \(s\) and wait \(\ln 2 / k\text{.}\) The ratio of the new value to the old one should be \(2\text{,}\) whatever \(s\) is:

    Subsection 5.1.2 Growth and decay: a slider

    Move the sliders and watch \(y = Ae^{kt}\) for \(t\) from 0 to 10. Click on a slider handle, then use the left and right arrow keys to move one step at a time. The red points mark each doubling time, or each halving time when \(k\) is negative. They are equally spaced along the \(t\) axis, and each is twice (or half) the one before. The cell uses plain decimal numbers rather than exact ones, which keeps a slider quick.
    🔗
    Things to try. A bigger \(|k|\) packs the red points closer together: \(k = 0.7\) doubles about once per time unit, because \(\ln 2 \approx 0.693\text{.}\) Make \(k\) negative for decay, where the curve flattens towards the axis but never touches it. At \(k = 0\) the quantity never changes, so the model has nothing to say.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.3 Finding A and k from two readings

    A sample of an isotope weighs \(750\) mg at the start and \(450\) mg after 6 days. The first reading is at \(t = 0\text{,}\) so \(A = 750\text{.}\) The second gives an equation for \(k\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 750e^{6k} = 450. \end{equation*}
    Sage’s solve is no help here. It takes a sixth root and offers all six of them, most of them complex numbers:
    🔗
    Take the logarithm yourself instead. Divide by \(750\) to get \(e^{6k} = \frac35\text{,}\) then take \(\ln\) of both sides, which gives \(6k = \ln\frac35\text{.}\) That is a linear equation, and Sage is happy with it. The half-life is \(\ln 2 / (-k)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    The mass at 10 days is \(750e^{10k}\text{.}\) There is also a shortcut that never finds \(k\) at all. Since \(e^{6k} = \frac35\text{,}\) we have \(e^{10k} = \left(e^{6k}\right)^{10/6} = \left(\frac35\right)^{5/3}\text{.}\) The two routes must agree:

    Subsection 5.1.4 A percentage per step is the same thing

    A savings account pays \(5\%\) a year on a deposit of \(2500\text{.}\) Each year multiplies the balance by \(1.05\text{,}\) so after \(t\) years it is \(2500 \times 1.05^t\text{.}\) Nothing forces \(t\) to be a whole number, so one line handles 7.5 years. That is an exponential too, with \(k = \ln 1.05\text{,}\) because \(1.05 = e^{\ln 1.05}\text{.}\) The two forms give the same answer:
    🔗
    So \(5\%\) a year is \(k = \ln 1.05 \approx 0.0488\text{,}\) not \(0.05\text{.}\) A rate of exactly \(k = 0.05\) is continuous compounding at \(5\%\text{,}\) which multiplies the balance by \(e^{0.05}\) each year, a little more than \(1.05\text{.}\) Interest paid \(n\) times a year multiplies the balance by \(\left(1 + \frac{0.05}{n}\right)^n\) each year. That creeps up towards the limit as \(n\) grows, but never passes it:
    🔗
    Sage can take the limit symbolically. This is the definition of \(e^r\) that continuous compounding rests on:
    🔗
    The doubling time at \(5\%\) a year is \(\ln 2 / \ln 1.05\text{:}\)
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.5 Solving for a time

    When does the isotope sample fall to \(100\) mg? Put the value in: \(750\left(\frac35\right)^{t/6} = 100\text{.}\) Divide by \(750\) to get \(\left(\frac35\right)^{t/6} = \frac{2}{15}\text{,}\) then take \(\ln\) of both sides. The power comes down in front, giving \(\frac{t}{6}\ln\frac35 = \ln\frac{2}{15}\text{.}\) Again that is a linear equation for \(t\text{:}\)
    🔗
    🔗
