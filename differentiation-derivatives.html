    Section 8.2 Derivatives, Gradients and Tangents

    First principles shows what a derivative is. In practice nobody works it out that way: the rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(ax^n\right) = nax^{n-1}\) does the job in one line, for whole-number, negative and fractional powers alike. This page uses the rule to find gradients, to build the equations of tangents and normals, to find points from a gradient, and to read a derivative as a rate of change. It ends with random tangent questions that Sage marks. The cells share their variables, so run them in order from the top.
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.1 The tangent at a point

    At the point \((a, f(a))\) the tangent has gradient \(f'(a)\) and passes through the point, so its equation is
    \begin{equation*} y - f(a) = f'(a)\,(x - a). \end{equation*}
    The slider moves the point along \(y = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 2\text{,}\) whose derivative is \(3x^2 - 12x + 9\text{.}\) First the setup:
    🔗
    Click on the slider handle and use the arrow keys to move in steps.
    🔗
    Try \(a = 1\) and \(a = 3\text{,}\) where the gradient is 0 and the tangent is flat. At \(a = 4\) the tangent crosses the curve a second time: a tangent touches the curve at its point, but it can meet it again somewhere else. Where the gradient is negative, the tangent slopes down.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.2 Powers of x

    Multiply by the power, then take 1 off it. A constant differentiates to 0. Write every term as a power of \(x\) before starting:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}, \qquad \frac{1}{x} = x^{-1}, \qquad \frac{1}{x^3} = x^{-3}, \qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} = x^{-1/2}, \qquad \sqrt[3]{x} = x^{1/3}. \end{equation*}
    Taking 1 off a negative power makes it more negative: \(x^{-3}\) becomes \(-3x^{-4}\text{,}\) and \(x^{-1/2}\) becomes \(-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-3/2}\text{.}\) Take \(y = 6\sqrt{x} + \dfrac{5}{x^3}\text{:}\)

    Subsection 8.2.3 The equation of a tangent

    Three things are needed: the point \((x_1, y_1)\) on the curve, the gradient \(m = \dfrac{dy}{dx}\) there, and then \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\text{.}\) Here is the tangent to \(y = 2x^3 - 5x + 1\) at \(x = 2\text{:}\)
    🔗
    The gradient is 19 and the point is \((2, 7)\text{.}\) Rearranged to \(y = mx + c\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Turn it round: where is the gradient 1? Put \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 1\) and solve. A cubic’s derivative is a quadratic, so there can be two points, each with its own tangent of gradient 1.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.4 Rates of change

    \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}\) is the rate at which \(V\) changes as \(t\) goes up. Say a tank holds \(V = 40 + 18t - 3t^2\) litres after \(t\) minutes of pumping in and out:
    🔗
    At \(t = 1\) the rate is 12: the tank is filling at 12 litres per minute. At \(t = 4\) it is \(-6\text{:}\) the volume is going down at 6 litres per minute. A question asking for the rate of decrease wants the 6, the size of the negative rate, with the sign explained in words.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.5 Normals

    The normal at a point is perpendicular to the tangent there, so its gradient is \(-\dfrac{1}{m}\text{:}\) two perpendicular gradients multiply to \(-1\text{.}\) Note the one exception, a flat tangent (\(m = 0\)), whose normal is the vertical line through the point. The helper below builds the normal as an equation; the example is \(y = x^2 + 6x - 2\) at \(x = 1\text{.}\)

    Subsection 8.2.6 A tangent that is given

    Sometimes the tangent’s equation is given and the point is not. Take \(y = 20 + 7x - x^2\) and the tangent \(y = 3x + c\text{,}\) where \(c\) is unknown. Gradient method: the tangent has gradient 3, so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 3\) at the point of contact.
    🔗
    The point is \((2, 30)\) and \(c = 30 - 3 \times 2 = 24\text{.}\) Discriminant method: the line meets the curve at exactly one point, so setting them equal gives a quadratic with a repeated root, and its discriminant \(b^2 - 4ac\) is 0.
    🔗
    Both methods give \(c = 24\text{.}\) The gradient method is shorter; the discriminant method works even when no derivative is available, for example for a line and a circle. With \(c = 24\) the repeated root can be read off too:
