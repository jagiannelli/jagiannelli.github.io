    Section 4.2 Circles

    The circle with centre \((a, b)\) and radius \(r\) is every point \((x, y)\) at distance \(r\) from the centre. By Pythagoras:
    \begin{equation*} (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2. \end{equation*}
    Almost every question about a circle and a line comes down to two things: completing the square to find the centre and radius, and solving a quadratic. Sage does both, and draws the picture, which is often the fastest way to see what is going on.
    🔗
    The cells on this page share their variables, so run them in order from the top. The helper functions in the first subsection are used all the way down.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.1 Some helper functions

    We work with \(x\) and \(y\) throughout, so declare them first. The %display latex line makes Sage typeset every answer from here on, so x^2 + y^2 == 25 shows as \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.1 Utility methods circle_equation() and centre_and_radius().

    The first builds the equation of a circle from its centre and radius. The second goes the other way: it divides through so the \(x^2\) term has coefficient 1, reads off \(g\text{,}\) \(f\) and \(c\) from \(x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0\text{,}\) and returns the centre \((-g, -f)\) and the radius \(\sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.2 Utility method meet_circle().

    The real points where a circle and a line meet: solve the two equations together and throw away any complex solutions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.3 Utility methods for points and lines.

    The gradient, length and midpoint of \(PQ\text{;}\) the line through \(P\) with gradient \(m\text{,}\) and the same line rearranged as \(y = mx + c\text{.}\) Last, the tangent at a point \(P\) on a circle: it is perpendicular to the radius at \(P\text{,}\) so its gradient is \(-1\) divided by the radius’s gradient. When the radius is horizontal the tangent is vertical, and that case is handled on its own.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.4 Utility methods signed_term() and circle_text().

    Sage prints (x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 == 25, which is fine for checking but not how we would write it. These two build the text of a circle’s equation in both of its usual forms.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.5 Utility method diagram().

    A picture drawn to scale: circles given as (centre, radius), lines given as equations, dashed segments between points, and named points labelled with their coordinates. set_aspect_ratio(1) is what keeps circles round and right angles looking like right angles.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.2 A line and a circle meet twice, once or never

    To find where the line \(y = mx + c\) meets a circle, put \(mx + c\) in for \(y\text{.}\) That leaves a quadratic in \(x\text{,}\) and its discriminant says how many answers there are:
    🔗
    • positive: two points (the line cuts the circle, and the part between the points is a chord);
      🔗
      🔗
    • zero: one point (the line is a tangent);
      🔗
      🔗
    • negative: none (the line misses).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Move the line. The circle is \(x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y = 12\) (centre \(A(3, -2)\text{,}\) radius \(5\)). One slider sets the gradient \(m\text{.}\) The other does not set the intercept \(c\text{.}\) It sets \(d\text{,}\) the distance from the centre to the line, signed so that a positive \(d\) puts the line above the centre. The intercept is worked out from the two:
    \begin{equation*} c = -2 - 3m + d\sqrt{1 + m^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Why not slide \(c\) itself? With \(m = 1\) the two tangents are at \(c = -5 \pm 5\sqrt2\text{.}\) Those numbers are irrational, so no slider step, however fine, ever lands on them: the discriminant jumps from positive to negative and never shows zero. The tangents are always at \(d = \pm 5\text{,}\) whatever the gradient, and those are on the grid.
    🔗
    Things to try. Set \(d = 5\) or \(d = -5\) and move \(m\text{:}\) the line stays a tangent, and the discriminant stays exactly zero. With \(m = 1\) and \(d = 5\) the intercept comes out as \(5\sqrt2 - 5\text{,}\) one of the two values above. Then try \(d = 4.5\) (two points) and \(d = 5.5\) (none). The discriminant always works out to \(4(1 + m^2)(25 - d^2)\text{,}\) so its sign is the sign of \(25 - d^2\text{:}\) the algebra (discriminant zero) and the geometry (distance from the centre equals the radius) give the same test. With \(m = 3/4\text{,}\) \(\sqrt{1 + m^2} = 5/4\) is rational, so every number in the working is too.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.3 Centre and radius

    A circle’s equation is usually given multiplied out, as in \(x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y = 12\text{.}\) Complete the square in \(x\) and in \(y\) separately:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 - 6x = (x - 3)^2 - 9, \qquad y^2 + 4y = (y + 2)^2 - 4, \end{equation*}
    so \((x - 3)^2 - 9 + (y + 2)^2 - 4 = 12\text{,}\) that is \((x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25\text{:}\) centre \((3, -2)\text{,}\) radius \(5\text{.}\) Note that the signs flip: \(-6x\) gives an \(x\)-coordinate of \(+3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Sage’s version, and a check that expanding the completed square gives the same equation back (Sage leaves \(13 = 25\) where we moved it across to make \(12\)):
    🔗
    Both forms as text:
    🔗
    Two traps. If the \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) terms have a number in front, divide through by it first (centre_and_radius does). And if \(g^2 + f^2 - c\) comes out zero or negative there is no circle at all: Sage shows that as an imaginary radius.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.4 Three circle properties

    Each of these turns a circle question into a straight-line question about gradients.
    🔗
    The angle in a semicircle is a right angle. If \(PQ\) is a diameter and \(R\) is any other point on the circle, then \(PR\) and \(QR\) are perpendicular: the product of their gradients is \(-1\text{.}\) Here \(P\) and \(Q\) are the ends of the horizontal diameter of our circle.
    🔗
    The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord. So the foot of that perpendicular is simply the midpoint of the chord. Here the chord is where \(y = x - 6\) cuts the circle; the gradient from the centre to the midpoint \(M\text{,}\) times the chord’s gradient \(1\text{,}\) is \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The tangent is perpendicular to the radius. So the gradient of the tangent at \(P\) is \(-1\) divided by the gradient of the radius. The tangents at the two ends of the chord:
    🔗
    A picture makes all three visible at once. The chord is blue, the two tangents red and green, and the radii to the ends of the chord are dashed.
    🔗
    A radius that is vertical gives a horizontal tangent, with gradient \(0\text{,}\) and the formula “minus one over the gradient” cannot be used. It is easy to miss without a sketch. The line \(y = 3\) touches the top of our circle; substituting it leaves a perfect square, which is what a repeated root, and so a tangent, looks like.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.5 Two circles touching

    Two circles touch when the distance between their centres is
    🔗
    • the sum of the radii: they touch externally, side by side;
      🔗
      🔗
    • the difference of the radii: they touch internally, one inside the other.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Our circle, centre \((3, -2)\) and radius \(5\text{,}\) against a circle with centre \((9, 6)\) and radius \(5\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \(AB = 10 = 5 + 5\text{,}\) so they touch externally, halfway along \(AB\) at \((6, 2)\text{.}\) A circle with the same centre \((9, 6)\) and radius \(15\) would touch ours internally, since \(15 - 5 = 10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.6 A circle through three points

    Take \(A(1, 1)\text{,}\) \(B(5, 3)\) and \(C(3, -3)\text{.}\) Look for a right angle first: if there is one, the angle in a semicircle makes the side opposite it a diameter, and the centre is that side’s midpoint.
    🔗
    The right angle is at \(A\text{,}\) so \(BC\) is the diameter:
    🔗
    Taking \(AB\) or \(AC\) as the diameter is the easy mistake; neither is opposite the right angle. Without a right angle, there is a method that always works: put all three points into \(x^2 + y^2 + ax + by + c = 0\) and solve the three linear equations for \(a\text{,}\) \(b\) and \(c\text{.}\) Sage does that directly.
    🔗
    That is \(x^2 + y^2 - 8x + 6 = 0\text{,}\) the same circle multiplied out.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.7 Tangents from a point outside the circle

    From a point outside a circle there are two tangents. Take the circle \(x^2 + y^2 - 10x + 20 = 0\) (centre \((5, 0)\text{,}\) radius \(\sqrt5\)) and the tangents through the origin. Every line through the origin is \(y = mx\text{,}\) with no constant term, and a tangent meets the circle exactly once, so the discriminant of the quadratic is zero. That gives an equation for \(m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For each gradient the quadratic is a perfect square, and its repeated root is the point of contact:
    🔗
    The picture shows a second method. Each radius to a point of contact is perpendicular to its tangent, so \(OTA\) is a right-angled triangle. \(OA = 5\) and the radius is \(\sqrt5\text{,}\) so by Pythagoras each tangent has length \(\sqrt{25 - 5} = 2\sqrt5\text{.}\)
