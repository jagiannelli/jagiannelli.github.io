    Section 9.1 Integrating Powers of x

    Integration is differentiation run backwards. Differentiating \(x^{n+1}\) gives \((n + 1)x^n\text{,}\) so going back,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n + 1} + C \qquad (n \ne -1): \end{equation*}
    🔗
    add 1 to the power, then divide by the new power. A sum is integrated one term at a time, and a number in front stays in front. The \(C\) is there because a constant differentiates to zero, so the reverse cannot tell which constant there was. The cells share their variables, so run them in order from the top.
    🔗

    Subsection 9.1.1 One term at a time

    Take \(\displaystyle\int \left(10x^4 + 6\sqrt{x} - \frac{9}{x^4}\right) dx\text{.}\) First the setup:
    🔗
    Step 1, index form. The rule needs every term as a number times a power of \(x\text{:}\) \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\) and \(\dfrac{9}{x^4} = 9x^{-4}\text{.}\) Sage keeps a term the way it was typed, but degree(x) reads off its power and coefficient(x, power) the number in front. operands() lists the terms of the sum.
    🔗
    Step 2, one term at a time. Add 1 to the power, then divide by the new power. Dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal: \(6x^{3/2} \div \frac{3}{2}\) is \(6 \times \frac{2}{3}\, x^{3/2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3, all at once. integrate(f, x) does the whole sum. It gives no \(+ C\text{:}\) Sage finds one integral, and you add the \(+ C\) yourself. Sage also writes a negative power back as a fraction; either form is fine on paper.
    🔗
    Step 4, the check. Differentiate the answer: it must give back what you integrated. bool(... == ...) says True when the two are equal.
    🔗

    Subsection 9.1.2 Why not n = -1, and what the + C means

    Ask Sage for \(\int x^y \, dx\text{,}\) with the letter \(y\) for the power, and it stops to ask “Is y equal to -1?”. Told that it is not (assume), it gives the rule; for \(\frac{1}{x}\) itself the answer is \(\ln x\) (Sage writes log), which belongs to a later course. (forget() clears the assumption.)
    🔗
    Now the \(+ C\) in a picture. Each value of \(C\) gives a curve, each the one above or below moved up or down. At any \(x\) they all have the same gradient (the short black lines at \(x = 1.2\) are parallel), and that gradient is \(10x^4 + 6\sqrt{x} - 9x^{-4}\text{.}\) So the integral is a whole family of curves; a point on the curve picks one of them (the next-but-one subsection).
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.1.3 A definite integral

    With limits, integrate(f, x, a, b) works out \(\Big[\,\text{integral}\,\Big]_a^b\text{,}\) the integral at \(b\) minus the integral at \(a\text{.}\) The \(C\) would be added and then taken away, so it is left out.     Subsection 9.1.4 Finding the curve from its gradient

    Integrating \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) gives every curve with that gradient, the \(+ C\) family above. A point on the curve picks one of them, so \(C\) becomes a number.     Subsection 9.1.5 Practice with random questions

    Each seed gives a new integral of three terms (the same seed always gives the same one): a fractional power, then a negative or a whole power, then a number. Now and then the question writes a root or a fraction (\(4\sqrt{x}\text{,}\) \(\frac{5}{x^4}\)); put it in index form first, on paper. Type the integral as an expression in x and leave the \(+ C\) out; write a fractional power in brackets, x**(3/2).
