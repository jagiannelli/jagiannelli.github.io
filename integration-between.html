    Section 9.3 Area Between a Line and a Curve

    When a straight line cuts a curve in two places, the two enclose a region. Its area is the integral of top minus bottom between the two crossings: \(\displaystyle\int_p^q (\text{top} - \text{bottom})\,dx\text{,}\) where \(p\) and \(q\) are the \(x\) values of the crossings. Two things decide it: the crossings (they are the limits) and which of the two is on top between them. There is a second way, often the one a written solution leads with: the area under the curve from \(p\) to \(q\) minus the area under the line, which is a trapezium (or a triangle). Both are below. The cells share their variables, so run them in order from the top.
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.1 Crossings, top and bottom, one integral

    Take the curve \(y = 14 + x - x^2\) and the line \(y = 2x + 8\text{.}\) First the setup:
    🔗
    Step 1, the limits. The curve and the line meet where their \(y\) values are equal. solve gives the \(x\) values: they are the limits, the smaller one first.
    🔗
    Step 2, which one is on top. Try an \(x\) between the limits, their middle. The bigger \(y\) value is the top one.
    🔗
    Step 3, a picture. plot(curve, (x, p, q), fill=straight) shades between the curve and the line (fill takes a function). The line and a little more of the curve are drawn too, so you can see the two cross at the ends of the shaded region.
    🔗
    Step 4, the area as one integral. The integrand is top minus bottom, here the curve minus the line. Both are above the \(x\)-axis here, but it would not matter if they were not: it is the gap between them that is integrated.

    Subsection 9.3.2 The other way, and two slips

    The area under the line from \(p\) to \(q\) is a trapezium: the average of the two end heights times the width. Take it away from the area under the curve (the curve is the top one here). The answer is the same as in step 4:
    🔗
    If the line met the \(x\)-axis at one of the crossings, the trapezium would be a triangle, \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}\text{,}\) and the method would not change.
    🔗
    Two slips to avoid. Bottom minus top gives a negative number, and an area is never negative. And the limits are \(x\) values: using the \(y\) values at the crossings (here \(2\) and \(12\)) as limits answers a different question altogether.

    Subsection 9.3.3 Practice with random questions

    Each seed gives a new line \(y = mx + k\) and a new curve, a quadratic, that cross at two points, with both above the \(x\)-axis between the crossings. The same seed always gives the same question. Sometimes the curve is on top and sometimes the line is, and about one time in three the line meets the \(x\)-axis at the left-hand crossing, so the area under it is a triangle rather than a trapezium. Find the crossings, sketch it and work it on paper first, then type the area as a number: a fraction like 125/6, or a decimal within 0.01 of it. The checker says whether it is right and, if not, what the usual slip looks like.

    Subsection 9.3.4 Stretch: where the top boundary changes

    Sometimes the region is not between the line and the curve all the way along. Part of it is between the curve and the axis, part between the line and the axis. Sketch it and decide where the boundary changes before you integrate anything.
    🔗
    The curve \(y = x^2\) and the line \(y = 6 - x\) meet at the point \((2, 4)\text{.}\) Find the area of the region bounded by the curve from \(x = 0\) to that point, the line from that point down to the \(x\)-axis, and the two axes.
